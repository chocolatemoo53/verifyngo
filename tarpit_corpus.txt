The old lighthouse keeper counted the waves from the tower window and wrote each number in a leather notebook he had carried for thirty years. Storms arrived without invitation, and the sea kept its own schedule regardless of human convenience. He believed that every tide returned something it had taken, though not always to the same shore.

Markets open early in the river district, where vendors arrange fruit into pyramids and call out prices that change with the weather. A crowd gathers near the fountain by midmorning, drawn by the smell of bread and the sound of a violin played by someone who learned long ago to ignore applause. Coins land in the open case like rain on a tin roof.

The library basement held maps of places that no longer existed, folded into rectangles and stored by year. Researchers requested them one at a time, wearing white gloves, copying coastlines onto fresh paper while the windows darkened outside. Somewhere above them, a radiator knocked twice and went quiet, as if embarrassed by the interruption.

Every autumn the town repainted the bridge in the same shade of green, arguing each year about whether the color had drifted over the decades. Old photographs suggested it had once been blue, but nobody alive could confirm it, and the paint store only stocked what the council ordered. Tradition survived on inertia more than affection.

The telescope arrived in eleven crates with instructions translated from three different languages. Students assembled it over a weekend, trading guesses about which bolt fit where, and by Sunday night it stood on the hill pointing at nothing in particular. The first clear sky brought the whole town up the path, breath steaming, waiting for Saturn.

Rivers carry more than water. They move silt and seeds, secrets and scrap metal, occasionally a piano that someone regretted owning. The current sorts everything by weight and patience, depositing the heavy things slowly and the light ones never. Fishermen read the surface the way scholars read manuscripts, finding meaning in ripples others call noise.

Bakeries fail for ordinary reasons: rent rises, ovens crack, children decline to inherit the hours. The good ones persist because somebody refuses to let the starter die, feeding it at dawn through illness and holidays and years when flour costs more than it should. Bread is a habit disguised as a product.

The train stopped running in March, but the timetable remained nailed to the platform, curling at the corners. Commuters drove now, past the crossing where the barrier arms hung open like shrugging shoulders. Wildflowers took the gravel bed between the ties, and by summer only the height of the weeds remembered where the rails had been.

Astronomers talk about deep time the way sailors talk about weather, casually and with respect. Light that left a star before there were words for stars arrives on schedule, indifferent to the small crowd gathered on a cold ridge to watch it land. Someone always asks how far away it is, and the answer makes the ridge feel thinner.
